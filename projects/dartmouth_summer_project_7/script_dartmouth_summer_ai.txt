They write code, diagnose diseases, and drive cars.
It feels like an unstoppable force that appeared out of nowhere.
But the real answer is far stranger.
Why did this world-altering technology start as a casual summer project?
The year was nineteen fifty-six.
Ten young scientists gathered on a quiet campus in New Hampshire.
They did not have supercomputers, internet databases, or massive funding.
They had only a bold, almost ridiculous belief.
Do you want to see how their overconfidence shaped your modern life?
Doctor John McCarthy was a young mathematics professor.
He worked at Dartmouth College.
He wanted to bring together the brightest minds in computing, mathematics, and psychology.
He wrote a proposal asking for a small grant.
What name did he choose to capture their wild ambition?
He wrote two words on the grant application.
Those words were artificial intelligence.
It was a clever marketing trick to distinguish their work from cybernetics.
Many of his colleagues hated the term.
Why did a simple marketing phrase capture the imagination of the world?
Doctor Marvin Minsky stood beside him.
He was a young researcher obsessed with neural networks.
They were joined by Doctor Claude Shannon, the legendary founder of information theory.
They believed they could solve the mind in just two months.
Why were these giants of science so incredibly naive?
They had just witnessed the rapid rise of digital computers.
In less than a decade, machines had gone from basic calculators to complex systems.
The scientists made a critical mistake.
Did they assume that logical reasoning was the hardest part of intelligence?
Human vision is effortless for us.
Walking is a simple task.
We spent millions of years evolving to see, balance, and move, but only a few thousand years learning formal logic.
Computers could solve math, but they could not recognize a cat.
Is it possible that we completely misunderstood what makes us smart?
Two researchers arrived from Pittsburgh.
Their names were Doctor Allen Newell and Doctor Herbert Simon.
They brought a program called the Logic Theorist that proved thirty-eight complex mathematical theorems.
Simon told his students they had built a thinking machine.
Why did this single success fuel such dangerous optimism?
Funding flowed into the universities.
Researchers made wild predictions.
They claimed that fully conscious machines would exist within a single generation.
But by the late nineteen sixties, the cracks began to show.
What happens when huge promises fail to deliver results?
The machines could play simple games.
They could not translate languages.
Translation software famously turned the spirit is willing into the vodka is good.
The software lacked real-world experience.
Why did early AI systems struggle so much with basic human communication?
The government cut off the funding.
The scientists returned to their homes.
Their overconfidence led to a sudden crash known as the artificial intelligence winter.
Yet, the core ideas developed during that summer never truly died.
How did a failed summer project lay the foundation for the modern world?
We face the same challenges today.
The technology is much faster.
But we are still trying to teach machines how to form abstract concepts.
The neural networks on your phone are descendants of their math.
Are we still making the same mistakes they made seventy years ago?
